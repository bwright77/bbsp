2014 — 2026 · A legacy Twelve years of work, in one report

Advancing equity in shared micromobility.

The Better Bike Share Partnership (BBSP) launched in 2014 as a one-of-a-kind collaborative between the City of Philadelphia, PeopleForBikes, and the National Association of City Transportation Officials (NACTO) to advance equity in shared micromobility. For more than a decade, BBSP successfully removed barriers to access and ridership for communities of color, increased diversity in the shared micromobility industry, and helped transform the way bike and scooter share programs design and implement their systems.

The legacy of BBSP is not just in its programs, but in the network of leaders and practitioners it strengthened across the country. BBSP created a collaborative community where cities, nonprofits, operators, and advocates could share ideas, learn from one another, and improve how shared mobility systems serve their communities. BBSP also developed and supported a new generation of transportation leaders from a wide range of backgrounds and experiences through initiatives like the Transportation Justice Fellowship.

Where it started

Equity, by design.
Not an afterthought.

Bike share took off in U.S. cities around 2008. From the start, BBSP saw who was being left out — and built a partnership to do something about it.

BBSP recognized there was a gap in who was being served by bike share when the first systems launched.

The City of Philadelphia knew they wanted to launch a bike share system built on a foundation of equity — not equity as an afterthought.

The City of Philadelphia wanted to make sure their riders looked like the majority-minority city they rode in. The partners came together around a common vision: to help bike and scooter share intentionally engage BIPOC and low-income communities in planning and implementation.

Philadelphia used early funding to install 20 stations in underserved neighborhoods, develop a cash payment option, and create a discounted pass program. That blueprint became the BBSP playbook.

How BBSP worked

Four pillars.

Each program reinforced the others. Grants funded critical outreach and education; storytelling spread what worked; peer convenings let practitioners trade ideas, present their work, and learn valuable insights; and the Transportation Justice Fellowship grew the next decade of BIPOC leaders.
PROGRAM · 01

Grantmaking

BBSP implemented a variety of grant programs including Challenge Grants, Emergency Response Grants, Mini Grants, and Living Lab Grants. BBSP grants were invaluable to the organizations and partnerships that received them. The unfortunate reality is that it is challenging to secure funding specifically for outreach and engagement programs. BBSP grants allowed shared micromobility systems to increase ridership among people of color and in socially vulnerable communities, allowed the systems to be a more consistent presence that communities could rely on, and once BBSP had invested in these organizations, funding from other sources became easier to secure.

$2.6M+across 125 grants · 59 cities
PROGRAM · 02

Storytelling

More than 500 stories across ten years documented successful equity work in shared micromobility from every angle — a public library of what inclusive shared bike and scooter systems look like in practice. BBSP's storytelling focused on uplifting the voices of those often overlooked in discussions of successful bike share programming and the stories helped seed ideas for more equity projects carried out throughout the country. One of BBSP's storytelling goals was to get the word out so that people could replicate the successful outreach and engagement work being done.

500+stories · permanent archive
PROGRAM · 03

Peer-to-Peer Learning Opportunities

Consistent and anticipated peer gatherings and study tours — like the annual Roundtable, an intimate work and play space with 40 people ranging from half-day workshops to multi-day study tours — plus regular peer social functions at national conferences like the NACTO's Designing Cities Conference and North American Bikeshare and Scootershare Association's (NABSA) annual conference.

Thirteen Roundtable events over 10 years brought shared micromobility practitioners together for peer exchange. The final 2026 Roundtable drew 40 practitioners from 20 cities to Tucson, AZ, for two days of collaboration, riding bikes and scooters, and sharing meals. Attendees discussed successes, tackled common hurdles, and brainstormed ways to ensure micromobility truly serves every member of a community. Success stories from BBSP Living Lab grantees and a dialogue between Tucson Vice Mayor Lane Santa Cruz and organizer Vanessa Gallego from Familias Unidas Ganando Accesibilidad (FUGA) reminded us what this work is all about: bike share that truly serves our communities.

13gatherings · 20 cities at Tucson 2026
PROGRAM · 04 · LED BY NACTO

Transportation Justice Fellowship

This intentional fellowship built community and skills among early- and mid-career people of color working to embed mobility justice in transportation. The fellowship supported and sustained these professionals as they did the heavy work to operationalize equity across the transportation field. Each year, between eight and 12 fellows were welcomed to the program. They participated in skills-building sessions, collaborated on small projects, and received one-on-one coaching from experts working at the intersection of transportation and social justice. Fellows also helped shape programming for NACTO's annual Designing Cities conference.

Forty-three fellows graduated over the life of the program — building a pipeline of diverse leaders whose impact will outlast the fellowship itself.

Stories from the ground

Living Labs.
Deep community investment.

Nine $200,000 grants across two cohorts and eight cities (Philadelphia twice) gave cities, transit agencies, nonprofit operators, and community organizations the time to pilot, pivot, and accelerate programs otherwise impossible. A true Living Lab, these projects tested ideas and found what really worked for their communities. We've selected four case studies — two from each cohort — that show what that time and trust made possible on the ground.

Living Lab · 2021–2023

Bikes for Belmont Cragin.

A neighborhood-led plan that turned bike infrastructure into community infrastructure.

The Northwest Center, a nonprofit in Belmont Cragin, used their Living Lab grant to empower youth, build community, and shine a light on transportation needs in the local neighborhood. Youth Transit Ambassadors (YTAs) ran workshops, organized community rides, participated in state capitol visits to inform their elected officials on the transportation needs in their neighborhood, and successfully advocated for bike lanes and bike share stations.

Story 03 / 04 · Pittsburgh, PA
Living Lab · 2024–2026

Advancing Equitable Outreach in Homewood.

POGOH, operated by Bike Share Pittsburgh, launched its Living Lab to address disparities in access to mobility in Homewood.

Through this initiative, the POGOH Living Lab worked alongside residents, schools, and community organizations to understand the real barriers to bike share use, build trust through consistent local engagement, create pathways for new riders — especially youth and families — and expand infrastructure to better serve community needs.

The detail that sticks

POGOH's Living Lab centered on a holistic, community-driven approach — pairing education, events, and partnerships to reduce barriers and build long-term engagement in Homewood. Through direct engagement led by POGOH outreach staff and community ambassadors, new station locations were identified — all within Homewood or a half-mile radius — strengthening connectivity and making the system more accessible for daily use. Expansion planning wasn't just about adding stations, but about placing them where they could have the greatest impact.

As a result, ridership to and from bike share stations in Homewood increased by 277% during the Living Lab grant period.

The coalition · Roundtables + Fellowship

The people behind the legacy.

BBSP's most durable output isn't a program — it's people, connected. Thirteen Roundtables over ten years brought city staff, transit agencies, nonprofit operators, and community organizers into one room: an intimate working space of 40 practitioners trading what worked, what didn't, and what to try next — reinforced by peer gatherings at NACTO's Designing Cities Conference and NABSA's annual conference.

The Transportation Justice Fellowship expanded that network. Forty-three early- and mid-career leaders of color — trained, coached, and connected across cohorts — are embedded throughout the transportation field, building meaningful careers, and operationalizing equity across North America.

When the final Roundtable convened in Tucson in 2026, it wasn't a farewell — it was a handoff. The funding ends; the coalition doesn't. BBSP's mission carries forward in every city where these practitioners and fellows now lead.

International legacy

A playbook for shared micromobility.

Beyond local wins for individual bike and scooter share systems, BBSP's lasting legacy is the way its ideas, tools, and partnerships continue shaping shared micromobility (SMM). With partners like NABSA, it changed how systems serve those who need a variety of mobility options most — and how they're designed across North America.

“BBSP has played a critical role in the fundamental transformation of SMM by helping cities address systemic and institutional barriers to access for low-income and BIPOC communities. Through funding, research, and technical assistance, BBSP has supported cities in testing new ideas, developing strategies, and learning from one another. As a result, the SMM industry has evolved to a place where equity is at the center of system design and operations, not as an afterthought, but as a core principle. That shift is in large part due to BBSP's leadership and sustained investment in this work.” — Waffiyyah Murray · Indego Program Manager · City of Philadelphia
What we built with partners
  • —Contributed to NABSA's Workforce Diversity Toolkit · a best practices guide for more diverse and inclusive workplaces.
  • —$100K+ funded Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Belonging (DEIB) content and scholarships for BIPOC attendees at the NABSA and NACTO conferences
  • —The Diverse Voices in Shared Micromobility video series · with NABSA.
  • —NABSA's City Share podcast · practitioner conversations from across the field.

The playbook travels.

In both directions: when Guadalajara's MiBici expanded with eight new stations sited by women riders through a gender-responsive planning guide, BBSP spread the word — putting the work in front of practitioners across North America so the next system could learn from it.

BBSP did more than support systems in their efforts to get more BIPOC and low-income people on shared bikes and scooters. It helped define what equity in shared micromobility can and should look like.

“The partnership's influence on the shared micromobility industry is evident and will live on through the network of practitioners who will continue to apply what they have learned as a result of being a part of the BBSP family. Biking is better in North America because of the Better Bike Share Partnership.” — Tangier Barnes Wright · Deputy Director of Shared Micromobility, PeopleForBikes

What's next

The work didn't end
when the funding did.

While BBSP has concluded, its impact continues through the people, programs, and practices it established. Cities, operators, advocates, and community leaders across North America continue building on this foundation every day.

BBSP's mission lives on in the 92% of systems that now offer discount programs — in the fellows who are now embedded throughout the transportation field working to operationalize equity across North America — in all the people of color who now see themselves in this space and are making careers out of it — in the community rides, the youth transit ambassadors, and the riders who got on a bike for the first time because existing barriers were removed.

If this work speaks to you — if you're a funder, a city, an operator, or an advocate who believes it should continue — we'd love to hear from you.
